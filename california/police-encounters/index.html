    Police Encounters — Detention, Identification, Questioning, Arrest, and Devices¶

    Research status: This chapter reviews the core federal constitutional and California authorities governing the recurring transition from peaceful First Amendment activity to police contact. The principal encounter sequence is covered at the doctrine level: consensual contact, Fourth Amendment seizure/detention, reasonable suspicion, identification demands, protective frisks, probable-cause arrest, Fifth Amendment/Miranda questioning, and the basic warrant rule for digital searches of a seized phone. Important edge questions remain separately queued, including when force converts a Terry stop into an arrest, compelled phone unlocking, anonymous-tip doctrine, detailed prolonged-stop rules, and remedies/qualified immunity.

    A First Amendment audit can quickly implicate several constitutional layers:

    • First Amendment: what expressive/recording activity is protected and what restrictions may government impose?
    • Fourth Amendment: when may government seize the person, frisk, arrest, seize property, or search it?
    • Fifth Amendment: when may government compel testimonial self-incrimination and what rules govern custodial interrogation?
    • Fourteenth Amendment: the incorporation bridge through which relevant federal Fourth/Fifth Amendment protections apply to California state and local actors, plus its own due-process/equal-protection protections.

    California Constitution article I, sections 13 and 15 supply parallel state search/seizure and self-incrimination protections. Their complete independent state-doctrine layer remains separately queued.

    Encounter map¶

    Analyze each stage separately:

    1. Consensual contact — may the person decline questions or terminate the encounter?
    2. Seizure/detention — has police conduct restrained liberty under the Fourth Amendment?
    3. Reason for stop / reasonable suspicion — for a California pedestrian stop within this project's current scope, what reason was stated before covered questioning, and what specific, articulable facts actually justified the detention when it began?
    4. Scope/duration — is the investigation reasonably related to its basis and pursued diligently?
    5. Identification — is identification merely requested, or does a specific law require it here?
    6. Frisk — is there a separate reasonable basis to believe the person is armed and dangerous?
    7. Arrest — did facts known to police establish probable cause?
    8. Questioning/self-incrimination — is the person merely detained, or in Miranda custody and being interrogated?
    9. Phone/camera — is police asking for consent, physically seizing the device, or searching digital contents?
    10. Afterward — release, property return, records preservation, and remedies are distinct questions.

    The legal threshold can change during the encounter. Do not collapse the entire interaction into one label.

    1. Consensual encounter versus Fourth Amendment seizure¶

    Police do not need reasonable suspicion merely to approach a person in a public place, identify themselves, ask questions, or request identification so long as the circumstances would communicate to a reasonable person that the person may decline the requests or otherwise terminate the encounter.

    A Fourth Amendment seizure occurs when physical force or a show of authority restrains liberty under the governing doctrine. A show-of-authority seizure generally requires submission to that authority.

    A useful field question is:

    Am I free to leave?

    This helps clarify the officer's asserted posture. It is not a magic constitutional test; courts examine the totality of circumstances.

    People v. Tacardon, 14 Cal.5th 235 (2022)¶

    The California Supreme Court rejected a bright-line rule that use of a spotlight necessarily detains a person. On the facts before it, the deputy approached a parked vehicle with a spotlight but without emergency lights, blocking the vehicle, drawing a weapon, or initially issuing a command. The court required a totality-of-circumstances analysis.

    Official sources:

    • https://supreme.courts.ca.gov/case/s264219-people-v-tacardon
    • https://www.courts.ca.gov/opinions/archive/S264219.PDF

    Field rule: police attention is not automatically a detention. Commands, physical restraint, blocking movement, threatening displays, emergency lights, weapons, multiple officers, tone, and other circumstances can matter.

    2. Terry detention — reasonable suspicion¶

    Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968) recognizes a limited investigative seizure short of arrest. Police may briefly detain a person when reasonable suspicion is supported by specific and articulable facts that criminal activity is afoot. Reasonable suspicion is less than probable cause, but more than a hunch.

    Official source:

    https://www.supremecourt.gov/pdfs/USReports/USREPORTS-392_PDFA.pdf

    People v. Flores, 15 Cal.5th 1032 (2024)¶

    The California Supreme Court held the facts there insufficient for reasonable suspicion. The opinion reinforces that a high-crime-area label is contextual rather than individualized suspicion and that unusual/evasive conduct must be assessed in the whole factual picture. It does not hold that evasive behavior can never matter.

    Official sources:

    • https://supreme.courts.ca.gov/case/s267522-people-v-flores
    • https://courts.ca.gov/opinion/citings-archive/2024-05-02/s267522

    Penal Code § 148(g) — recording bridge¶

    California Penal Code § 148(g) provides that photographing or recording a public/peace officer, while the officer is in a public place or the recorder is in a place the recorder has a right to be, does not in and of itself:

    • violate § 148(a);
    • create reasonable suspicion to detain; or
    • create probable cause to arrest.

    Primary source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=148

    California pedestrian-stop notice rule — Vehicle Code § 2806.5¶

    For this project's present facility-audit scope, the relevant part of Vehicle Code § 2806.5 is its pedestrian-stop rule. Effective January 1, 2024, the statute requires a peace officer making a traffic or pedestrian stop, before engaging in questioning related to a criminal investigation or traffic violation, to state the reason for the stop. The traffic-stop branch exists but is not developed further in this Field Guide at this stage. The officer must also document the reason on any resulting citation or police report.

    The statute has an exception when the officer reasonably believes withholding the reason is necessary to protect life or property from an imminent threat.

    Section 2806.5 is a stop-notice rule. It does not convert a consensual police approach into a detention; first determine whether a traffic or pedestrian stop has actually occurred.

    Primary source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=VEH&sectionNum=2806.5

    In People v. Valle, A169080 (Cal. Ct. App. Sept. 18, 2024), the published First District Court of Appeal described § 2806.5 as requiring notice to the detainee of the objective reason for a stop before the covered questioning. Valle arose from a traffic stop; the Field Guide retains it only for the statute's interpretation and does not use it to expand the project's traffic-stop scope. The court also explained that the statute does not alter the federal Fourth Amendment analysis governing whether the stop itself is objectively justified. Valle notes that proposed language making a failure to state the reason grounds for a suppression motion was removed before enactment.

    Official California Judicial Branch source:

    https://courts.ca.gov/opinion/published-extended-post/2024-09-18/a169080

    Reason for the stop is not the same as the full reasonable-suspicion record¶

    Section 2806.5 creates a California notice requirement. It does not say that the officer must recite every observation, caller statement, inference, or item of evidence that may later be used to establish reasonable suspicion.

    The Fourth Amendment question remains whether sufficient specific and articulable facts actually existed when the seizure began.

    Useful questions are therefore:

    Am I free to leave?

    Am I being detained?

    What is the reason for the stop?

    What crime do you reasonably suspect me of committing?

    What specific facts are you relying on?

    These questions help preserve the encounter timeline and the officer's contemporaneous explanation. Absent the statutory exception, failure to state the reason before questioning covered by § 2806.5 violates the stop-notice requirement. That does not by itself establish that the detention lacked Fourth Amendment reasonable suspicion; remedies are a separate layer. Refusal to provide every underlying fact on scene likewise does not, by itself, establish that reasonable suspicion was absent.

    3. Reasonable suspicion must exist when the seizure begins¶

    A Terry stop must be justified at inception. Facts learned during a lawful stop may expand the investigation or eventually supply probable cause, but later discoveries do not retroactively create reasonable suspicion for an earlier unlawful seizure.

    Existence and disclosure are separate questions. For a California pedestrian stop within this project's current scope, Vehicle Code § 2806.5 may require the officer to state the reason before covered questioning, but stating a reason cannot manufacture reasonable suspicion that did not already exist when the seizure began.

    A useful timeline is:

    T1: what facts were known when the seizure began?
    T2: what new facts lawfully developed?
    T3: did probable cause later arise?

    Do not project everything eventually learned backward to the beginning of the encounter.

    4. Scope and duration¶

    Under United States v. Sharpe, 470 U.S. 675 (1985), there is no universal stopwatch for Terry detentions. The inquiry includes whether police diligently pursued investigative means likely to confirm or dispel suspicion reasonably quickly under the circumstances.

    Official source:

    https://www.supremecourt.gov/pdfs/USReports/USREPORTS-470_PDFA.pdf

    For traffic stops, Rodriguez v. United States, 575 U.S. 348 (2015) holds that police may not prolong the traffic mission to pursue unrelated investigation absent an independent legal basis.

    Official source:

    https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/575bv.pdf

    Field rule: a stop lawful at inception can become unlawful if its scope or continued duration is no longer justified.

    5. Identification — request, statutory duty, silence, booking, and false information are different questions¶

    Avoid both slogans:

    You never have to identify in California.

    and

    If you're detained, you have to show ID.

    The correct sequence is:

    1. Is the encounter consensual, a lawful detention, or an arrest?
    2. Is the officer merely asking for identification?
    3. Is there a current California statute requiring specified identifying information in this context?
    4. Is this a driver, citation, booking, specialized-facility, probation/parole, or other special setting?
    5. Is the issue silence/refusal, or false information? Those are not equivalent.

    Hiibel does not itself create a California duty¶

    Hiibel v. Sixth Judicial District Court, 542 U.S. 177 (2004) upheld application of Nevada's own stop-and-identify statute during a valid Terry stop. As authoritatively construed, Nevada required Hiibel to state his name. The Court found that requirement constitutional on those facts and rejected the Fifth Amendment claim because stating his name did not present a reasonable danger of incrimination there.

    Official source:

    https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/542bv.pdf

    Hiibel establishes that a state may enact a qualifying stop-and-identify requirement. It does not legislate one for California and does not hold that identity can never have Fifth Amendment implications.

    Historical California context — Kolender v. Lawson¶

    In Kolender v. Lawson, 461 U.S. 352 (1983), the Supreme Court invalidated California's former requirement that a stopped person provide credible and reliable identification because the scheme was unconstitutionally vague and gave excessive enforcement discretion. Hiibel itself discusses that history.

    This historical case does not mean all modern California identification requirements are invalid. Current specialized statutes still control their own settings.

    United States v. Landeros, 913 F.3d 862 (9th Cir. 2019)¶

    Published Ninth Circuit authority held that a passenger's refusal to identify himself, where officers lacked reasonable suspicion that he had committed an offense, did not itself create reasonable suspicion and did not justify prolonging the traffic stop to investigate him.

    Official Ninth Circuit opinion:

    https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2019/01/11/17-10217.pdf

    The important distinction is request versus compulsion. An officer may ask without necessarily having authority to impose criminal consequences for refusal.

    California published cases — § 148 is not a generic silence offense¶

    People v. Quiroga, 16 Cal.App.4th 961 (1993)¶

    The published Court of Appeal decision drew several important lines under Penal Code § 148:

    • slow compliance and verbal argument did not automatically amount to § 148 obstruction;
    • refusing to give a name after arrest while being transported did not itself violate § 148 because the arrest had already been effected and booking had not begun;
    • refusing identity during a felony booking interview could violate § 148 because it actually delayed the booking process;
    • the court distinguished misdemeanor/infraction identification contexts, for which California statutes separately address identification and release/custody procedures.

    The current Judicial Council CALCRIM No. 2656 cites Quiroga under Verbal Resistance or Obstruction and specifically describes the nondisclosure proposition as identity following arrest for a felony, not misdemeanor.

    Official Judicial Council source:

    https://courts.ca.gov/advisory-body/criminal-jury-instructions-advisory-committee

    Full published opinion text (reporter reproduction):

    https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/4th/16/961.html

    In re Chase C., 243 Cal.App.4th 107 (2015)¶

    This published Court of Appeal decision applied Quiroga in a police-investigation setting. The court held that, on the facts before it, the minor's refusal to identify himself while detained/postarrest but before booking did not constitute § 148 obstruction; the opinion described that refusal as protected Fifth Amendment silence. It distinguished the booking stage and false identification.

    The case is also important more generally because it emphasized that refusal to cooperate becomes criminal under § 148 only when it actually obstructs lawful police activity; verbal protest, slow compliance, or refusal cannot simply be relabeled obstruction without the statute's elements.

    Published opinion text:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2015/d067787.html

    Limits of Quiroga / Chase C.¶

    Do not turn these cases into:

    The Fifth Amendment always lets every California detainee refuse every identification demand.

    That would conflict with the context-specific structure of Hiibel and California statutes. The narrower California proposition is:

    Silence or refusal to identify is not automatically Penal Code § 148 obstruction. Identify the actual statutory duty and stage of the encounter. Booking and specialized statutory contexts can be different.

    Penal Code § 148.9 — false identity is separate¶

    Penal Code § 148.9 makes it a misdemeanor, under its stated elements, to falsely represent or identify oneself as another or fictitious person to specified officers upon lawful detention or arrest for the purpose of evading process or proper identification.

    Primary source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=148.9

    Section 148.9 tells a detainee what not to do. Its text does not itself say every lawfully detained pedestrian must affirmatively produce ID.

    Carrying physical identification is a separate question¶

    The current California sweep has not identified a general statute requiring an ordinary pedestrian merely existing in public to carry a physical identification card.

    That question is separate from whether an officer may ask for a name, whether a specific statute requires identifying information in a specialized context, or whether a person is subject to an arrest/booking/citation procedure.

    A call for service, an officer's desire to "investigate," or an officer's request for identification does not itself create a general carry-ID statute. Facts reported in a call can still matter to reasonable-suspicion analysis, and a specific statutory context can create a real identification duty.

    The project therefore distinguishes:

    carry ID → produce a document → state identifying information → do not provide false identity

    Those are different legal questions.

    Scope of this conclusion: the present field rule concerns the absence of a general carry-ID statute and a general statutory stop-and-identify rule. It does not yet resolve every California case-law question about whether, during an otherwise lawful detention, an officer may direct a person to produce proof of identity that the person actually possesses. Older published California cases addressing identification production and identification searches are queued for reconciliation before the guide states a broader categorical rule.

    Drivers — Vehicle Code § 12951¶

    Driving is different. Vehicle Code § 12951 requires a driver to possess the valid license when driving on a highway and present it on demand to a peace officer enforcing the Vehicle Code.

    Primary source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=VEH&sectionNum=12951

    Infraction-arrest citation — Penal Code § 853.5¶

    Section 853.5 supplies identification/fingerprint procedures when an officer is issuing a written promise to appear after an infraction arrest. It is not a general pre-arrest pedestrian stop-and-identify statute.

    Primary source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=853.5

    Vehicle Code § 40302¶

    Section 40302 supplies additional identity/custody rules after arrest for specified nonfelony Vehicle Code violations.

    Primary source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=VEH&sectionNum=40302

    Current California field rule¶

    This sweep has not identified a current general California pedestrian stop-and-identify statute equivalent to Nevada's statute in Hiibel. That is not the same as saying identification can never be required. Determine the specific current authority and context. Silence/refusal is not automatically § 148 obstruction, but false identity can independently violate § 148.9.

    Useful question:

    What law requires me to provide identification under these circumstances?

    And regardless of whether a duty exists:

    Do not give a false identity.

    6. Stop does not automatically authorize a frisk¶

    A Terry detention and Terry frisk are analytically distinct. A protective frisk requires a reasonable basis to believe the detained person is armed and dangerous. Its purpose/scope is protective rather than a general evidence search.

    Useful question:

    Are you frisking me because you believe I am armed and dangerous? What facts support that?

    Do not physically resist. Plain-feel/container doctrine remains queued.

    7. Arrest — probable cause is a different threshold¶

    An arrest ordinarily requires probable cause to believe the person committed or is committing an offense.

    • reasonable suspicion: supports a limited investigative detention;
    • probable cause: ordinarily supports arrest.

    Devenpeck v. Alford, 543 U.S. 146 (2004)¶

    The Supreme Court held that warrantless-arrest legality is an objective probable-cause inquiry. The offense objectively supported by known facts need not be the same offense the officer announced.

    Official source:

    https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/543bv.pdf

    Thus:

    Asking What offense? is excellent record-building, but an officer's verbal answer is not necessarily the complete later probable-cause analysis.

    8. Fourth Amendment detention is not the same as Miranda custody¶

    A Terry detainee is not free to leave for Fourth Amendment purposes but is not automatically in custody for Miranda purposes.

    In Berkemer v. McCarty, 468 U.S. 420 (1984), the Supreme Court explained that ordinary traffic stops resemble Terry stops and generally are noncustodial for Miranda even though they significantly restrict movement. Miranda applies when the treatment reaches the degree associated with formal arrest.

    Official source:

    https://www.supremecourt.gov/pdfs/USReports/USREPORTS-468_PDFA.pdf

    Field distinction:

    4A: Am I seized, and what justifies that seizure?

    5A/Miranda: Am I in Miranda custody and being interrogated?

    A person may answer yes to the first and no to the second.

    9. Fifth Amendment privilege and Miranda¶

    The Fifth Amendment protects against compelled testimonial self-incrimination. Malloy v. Hogan, 378 U.S. 1 (1964) applies that privilege against the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. California Constitution article I, section 15 provides a parallel state privilege.

    Miranda is a procedural doctrine protecting the privilege during custodial interrogation. Failure to give Miranda warnings does not automatically make the underlying detention or arrest unlawful.

    Clear invocation is the safer rule¶

    In Berghuis v. Thompkins, 560 U.S. 370 (2010), the Court held in the Miranda context that invocation of the right to remain silent must be unambiguous; prolonged silence did not itself invoke the right on those facts.

    Official source:

    https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/560bv.pdf

    In Salinas v. Texas, 570 U.S. 178 (2013), a plurality concluded that a person in a voluntary, noncustodial interview could not rely on silence alone on the facts there because he had not expressly invoked the privilege.

    Official source:

    https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/570bv.pdf

    Because Salinas is plurality-limited and invocation doctrine is context-dependent, the Field Guide should not say silence never invokes the Fifth Amendment. The safer operational advice is:

    If you intend to invoke, say so clearly rather than expecting silence alone to communicate the invocation.

    Useful language:

    I invoke my right to remain silent. I do not want to answer investigative questions.

    No exact incantation is required; clarity is the point.

    Invocation does not automatically terminate a lawful detention or erase a specific identification duty that is otherwise constitutionally enforceable.

    10. Hiibel's Fifth Amendment holding is narrow¶

    Hiibel rejected the Fifth Amendment challenge because on those facts stating the detainee's name did not create a reasonable danger of incrimination. The opinion did not hold that identity can never be testimonial/incriminating.

    For California, ask in order:

    1. Is there a lawful detention?
    2. Is there a current law requiring the particular information?
    3. What exactly is demanded — name, document, address, DOB, fingerprint, etc.?
    4. Does the Fifth Amendment create an additional issue on these facts?

    11. Phones and cameras — seizure is not digital search¶

    Keep separate:

    1. consent to hand over the device;
    2. physical seizure;
    3. legal basis for seizure;
    4. search of digital contents;
    5. warrant/consent/exception;
    6. compelled unlocking.

    Riley v. California, 573 U.S. 373 (2014)¶

    The Supreme Court held that search incident to arrest does not ordinarily authorize police to search digital information on a cellphone merely because the phone was seized from a lawfully arrested person. Police generally need a warrant to search digital contents, subject to recognized case-specific exceptions such as exigency.

    Official source:

    https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/573BV.pdf

    Critical distinction: Riley does not mean police can never physically seize a phone. Seizure/retention and digital-content search require separate analysis.

    Useful language:

    I do not consent to seizure or search of my phone. If you are ordering me to hand it over, I am complying under protest and not consenting.

    Do you have a warrant to search its digital contents?

    Do not physically fight over the device.

    Compelled unlocking — still RESEARCH¶

    Passcodes, biometrics, encryption, the testimonial-act doctrine, warrants/orders, and the foregone-conclusion doctrine require a dedicated California/Ninth Circuit review. Do not use slogans such as passcode = Fifth Amendment or biometrics are always unprotected.

    12. Practical encounter script — evidence-preserving, not magic words¶

    Initial contact¶

    Am I free to leave?

    If no¶

    Am I being detained?

    What is the reason for the stop?

    What offense do you reasonably suspect me of committing?

    What specific facts are you relying on?

    If ID is demanded¶

    What legal authority requires me to provide identification under these circumstances?

    Never provide false/fictitious identity.

    If a frisk/search begins¶

    I do not consent to any search.

    Are you frisking me because you believe I am armed and dangerous?

    If substantive questioning begins¶

    I invoke my right to remain silent. I do not want to answer investigative questions.

    If a device is demanded¶

    I do not consent to seizure or search of my device. If you are ordering me to hand it over, I am complying under protest and not consenting.

    Physical nonresistance and legal consent are different things.

    13. Common bad field rules¶

    Bad slogan Better rule
    If I'm not under arrest, I can leave. A Terry detainee may be temporarily not free to leave without being under formal arrest.
    If I'm detained, they have to Mirandize me. Fourth Amendment detention and Miranda custody are different thresholds.
    Hiibel says I have to identify whenever detained. Hiibel upheld Nevada's statute; identify California authority for the actual context.
    The officer has to tell me all of the RAS immediately. For a pedestrian stop within the guide's current scope, California § 2806.5 requires the reason for the stop before covered questioning, subject to its exception. That is different from requiring an on-scene recital of every fact underlying reasonable suspicion.
    California has no stop-and-ID law, so I can lie. Whether affirmative ID is required is separate from § 148.9's false-ID offense.
    Refusing my name is automatically obstruction. California published cases distinguish silence/refusal from actual § 148 obstruction and distinguish booking from earlier stages.
    A legal stop automatically allows a frisk. A frisk requires a separate armed-and-dangerous basis.
    No Miranda warning means the arrest is illegal. Arrest legality is primarily a 4A probable-cause question; Miranda concerns custodial interrogation/statement use.
    Arrest means police can search my phone. Riley generally requires a warrant for digital contents; seizure and search are separate.

    15. Research still required¶

    • when handcuffs, drawn weapons, prone positioning, transport, or force converts Terry stop into arrest;
    • anonymous/911/security/employee reports and informant reliability;
    • mistakes of law/fact and collective knowledge;
    • full traffic-stop passenger doctrine;
    • further California construction of Vehicle Code § 2806.5 as applied to pedestrian stops, including what level of specificity satisfies "the reason for the stop," the imminent-threat exception, and remedies beyond the limited Valle discussion;
    • reconcile older California identification-production/search cases, including People v. Long, People v. Loudermilk, People v. Garcia, and later applications, before stating a categorical rule about compelled production of possessed identification during a lawful detention;
    • frisk/plain-feel/container doctrine;
    • consent searches;
    • physical seizure/retention of phones/cameras;
    • digital warrants/particularity and exigency;
    • compelled passcodes/biometrics/encryption;
    • complete California Constitution article I §§ 13 and 15 independent doctrine;
    • full Miranda custody/interrogation/public-safety/waiver/reinitiation/right-to-counsel doctrine;
    • Sixth Amendment attachment;
    • suppression/remedies, §1983, qualified immunity, Bane Act, municipal liability, damages;
    • excessive force.
