    Disturbing the Peace and Disorderly Conduct¶

    California does not have one generic offense that means "someone was uncomfortable with your behavior." Two provisions especially worth separating are Penal Code §§ 415 and 647.

    Penal Code § 415 — disturbing the peace¶

    Section 415 criminalizes three categories of conduct:

    1. unlawfully fighting in public or challenging another person in public to fight;
    2. maliciously and willfully disturbing another person by loud and unreasonable noise; or
    3. using offensive words in public that are inherently likely to provoke an immediate violent reaction.

    Primary source: California Penal Code § 415

    Field rule¶

    Under the project's standing scenario, the assumed auditor is not fighting, challenging anyone to fight, creating loud and unreasonable noise, or using words assumed to satisfy § 415(3).

    A government employee, contractor, visitor, security guard, or private individual becoming angry, offended, uncomfortable, or confrontational does not by itself supply one of § 415's statutory categories. The analysis remains focused on the auditor's own conduct. Another person's reaction may still be relevant context when § 415(3) is actually at issue, but the reaction alone does not substitute for the statute's elements.

    Therefore, if § 415 is asserted against a peaceful auditor, identify which subsection and which conduct is alleged to satisfy it rather than accepting "you are causing a disturbance" as though that were the statutory text.

    The third subsection requires separate case-law treatment because constitutional "fighting words" doctrine and context matter. That case-law section remains to be researched and added.

    Penal Code § 647 — disorderly conduct¶

    Section 647 is a list of specifically defined offenses. It is not a free-floating power to arrest a person merely for behaving unusually or making another person uncomfortable.

    Primary source: California Penal Code § 647

    Loitering provisions relevant to common audit claims¶

    Section 647(d) concerns loitering in or around a public toilet for the purpose of engaging in or soliciting a lewd, lascivious, or unlawful act.

    Section 647(h) concerns a person who loiters, prowls, or wanders on the private property of another without visible or lawful business with the owner/occupant. The statute defines "loiter" there as delaying or lingering without a lawful purpose on the property and for the purpose of committing a crime as opportunity may be discovered.

    Under the standing scenario, § 647(h) will ordinarily fall outside scope because the auditor is not silently assumed to be on another's private property without lawful purpose and for the purpose of committing a crime as opportunity may be discovered. Do not rely on public ownership alone: a government office can occupy leased or privately owned property, so analyze the actual property/access facts when they matter.

    Field rule¶

    "Loitering" should never be treated as a generic synonym for "standing around longer than an employee wants."

    When a public employee or officer uses the word, identify the exact statute, ordinance, regulation, or facility rule being invoked and compare its actual elements with the conduct observed.

    Useful factual questions¶

    • Which statute are you referring to?
    • Which subsection?
    • What specific conduct are you saying satisfies that subsection?
    • Are you saying the issue is noise, fighting, threatening words, obstruction, or something else?

    Research still required¶

    This chapter still needs controlling case law on:

    • fighting words;
    • offensive speech directed at police;
    • heckler's-veto doctrine;
    • unreasonable-noise standards;
    • protected criticism versus actual threats;
    • local disorderly-conduct ordinances that may appear in facility encounters.
