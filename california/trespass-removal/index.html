    Trespass and Removal from Public Agencies¶

    Research status: Penal Code §§ 601-603 and the complete current § 602 / § 602.x family have been reviewed at the primary-text level. A first controlling-case pass has also been completed for § 602(m), § 602(q), and the common intentional-interference structure of § 602.1. Core public-forum doctrine is now reviewed in public-forum-doctrine.md; area-specific forum classifications, local ordinances, facility-specific rules, and several other trespass subsections still require separate research.

    This chapter addresses the recurring audit problem in which a government employee or peace officer treats you were asked to leave as though it were a complete criminal-trespass rule. California's trespass statutes are more specific than that.

    Under the project's standing scenario, the auditor begins lawfully present in the specific public area and is not assumed to be obstructing, intimidating, threatening, or otherwise committing an independent offense. A later order to leave is therefore treated as a new fact: the guide analyzes the authority for the order and the consequences of remaining rather than retroactively assuming the auditor was already trespassing.

    Short field rule¶

    Within the California trespass statutes reviewed here, there is no general formula that reduces criminal trespass in an ordinary public-agency interior to:

    A government employee asked you to leave, therefore remaining is automatically criminal trespass.

    Identify the actual statute or other legal authority, then test its elements against the facts. Different provisions turn on materially different predicates: whether property is open to the general public, whether a public building is closed, whether someone is intentionally obstructing agency business, whether a person entered and occupied property without consent, whether a secured area is posted, or whether screening was intentionally avoided.

    A lawful policy or facility restriction can still matter even when a particular trespass subsection does not fit. Conversely, the existence or violation of a policy does not by itself establish every element of a criminal-trespass offense.

    Important separate question: open to the public is an access description, not a forum classification. A public-facing lobby may be open for agency business while still being a nonpublic or limited forum for expressive activity. Forum status must be analyzed separately, often area by area within the same facility. See public-forum-doctrine.md.

    Core distinctions inside § 602¶

    § 602(m) — entering and occupying means more than transient presence¶

    The statutory text uses the conjunctive phrase entering and occupying real property or a structure without consent.

    This is no longer merely a textual inference. In In re Catalano, 29 Cal.3d 1 (1981), the California Supreme Court explained that the predecessor subdivision requires occupation of the property, a nontransient, continuous type of possession; the subdivision was later renumbered to current § 602(m) without substantive change to this language.

    The published Ninth Circuit decision Edgerly v. City & County of San Francisco, 599 F.3d 946 (9th Cir. 2010) applied that rule to probable cause. Officers knew that Edgerly had been on fenced property for only a matter of minutes. The Ninth Circuit held those facts did not give a reasonable officer probable cause under the entering-and-occupying subdivision. The court nevertheless found probable cause under a different trespass statute, § 602.8(a), because the property was fenced and posted. That second holding is an important warning not to turn the § 602(m) limitation into a blanket no trespass rule.

    Current Judicial Council CALCRIM No. 2931 likewise instructs that the prosecution must prove that, after entering without consent, the defendant occupied some part of the property continuously until removed.

    Doctrine-reviewed field rule: Mere momentary or transient presence is not enough for § 602(m)'s occupying element. Another trespass provision can independently fit the same facts.

    § 602(o) — property not open to the general public¶

    Section 602(o) expressly concerns land, real property, or structures not open to the general public. Its refusal-to-leave language should not be quoted as though it were a universal rule for an ordinary public counter, lobby, library floor, or other place currently open to the public.

    Areas within a generally public-facing property can still be separately nonpublic. Federal cases applying California law have treated the particular area at issue—not merely the character of the larger property—as important. This proposition remains below DOCTRINE-REVIEWED for our public-agency scenario until the California case layer is completed.

    Caution: do not overstate § 602(o)'s constitutional-savings language¶

    The text broadly contains a labor-activity exception. It also contains constitutional-activity language in the sentence addressing housing-authority property. Courts have not spoken with one clear published voice about whether that constitutional language operates as a general exception to all of subdivision (o) or specifically as part of the housing-authority rule.

    A federal district court in Cuviello v. Cal Expo read the constitutional phrase as linked to the housing-authority clause rather than as a subdivision-wide exemption. By contrast, the 2026 Court of Appeal opinion People v. Hsiung read the phrase broadly while rejecting a First Amendment challenge—but that discussion appears in Part III, which the Court of Appeal expressly excluded from publication. It is therefore not precedential California authority.

    Field Guide rule: Do not presently claim that § 602(o) contains a settled subdivision-wide constitutional exemption. Analyze the actual constitutional right independently and continue the case-law research.

    § 602(q) — public buildings after closing¶

    Section 602(q) expressly addresses a public building of a public agency, but its text is limited to hours when the building is regularly closed to the public. It also requires a request from a regularly employed guard, watchperson, or custodian and circumstances that would indicate to a reasonable person that the person has no apparent lawful business to pursue.

    Published California authority confirms that those limitations matter. In Parrish v. Municipal Court, 258 Cal.App.2d 497 (1968), the Court of Appeal considered the predecessor-lettered version of this same public-building closing-hours rule. A welfare-rights advocate remained in a county welfare building hours after its regular closing. The court treated regular closing, a qualifying request, and the apparent lawful business inquiry as substantive predicates and held that the latter uses an objective reasonable-person standard. The opinion also relied on In re Bacon for the proposition that expressive/grievance activity that may be lawful during open hours does not itself require a public building to remain open after its regular closing.

    Doctrine-reviewed field rule: § 602(q) is a closing-hours statute, not a general daytime removal provision. The apparent lawful business component is objective rather than whatever the person personally believes.

    Case source:

    • Parrish v. Municipal Court, 258 Cal.App.2d 497 (Cal. Ct. App. 1968): https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2d/258/497.html

    § 602(y) — security screening is its own rule¶

    Section 602(y) concerns intentionally avoiding submission to the screening and inspection of one's person and accessible property when entering or reentering a courthouse or city/county/state building, where the entrances carry the required notice. It is not a generic prohibition on recording or peaceful presence after successfully passing security.

    Screening is not the same thing as a visitor sign-in or ID requirement¶

    A visitor log, badge procedure, appointment check, request to state a destination, or demand to show identification is a separate access-policy question. Section 602(y)'s text does not itself say that refusal to write one's name in a binder, state a purpose, or produce identification is criminal trespass.

    A government office may still be able to impose reasonable, viewpoint-neutral visitor conditions under other lawful authority, especially in a nonpublic or limited-purpose forum. The legal analysis therefore has two steps:

    1. Is the visitor condition itself lawful for this particular area and purpose?
    2. If a person refuses it, what statute or other authority actually supports exclusion, detention, or arrest?

    Do not collapse the facility has a sign-in policy into § 602(y) makes refusal a crime.

    Penal Code § 602.1 — interference with business/public-agency operations¶

    Primary source:
    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=602.1

    § 602.1(b) — public agency open to the public¶

    For a public agency open to the public, § 602.1(b) requires:

    1. intentional interference with lawful public-agency business;
    2. accomplished by obstructing or intimidating employees carrying on the business or people there to transact business; and
    3. refusal to leave after a qualifying request from the office manager/supervisor or a peace officer acting at that person's request.

    The statute does not say employee asks + refusal = offense.

    Under this project's standing scenario, the auditor is not blocking access, physically interfering, threatening, creating unreasonable noise, or invading personal space. That makes the statute's actual intentional interference / obstruction / intimidation language especially important.

    Published appellate guideposts — policy violation is not automatically the crime¶

    In Dubner v. City & County of San Francisco, 266 F.3d 959 (9th Cir. 2001), a photographer/legal observer was arrested near the Moscone Center under § 602.1. The Ninth Circuit described § 602.1 as requiring both intentional interference and refusal to leave and expressly rejected the argument that the intent-to-interfere element could simply be inferred from refusal. The record showed no deliberate obstruction or intimidation: Dubner was photographing, conversing, and positioned away from the doors.

    The precise statutory wording quoted in Dubner concerned the business-establishment version of § 602.1 rather than today's public-agency subdivision (b), so the case should not be overstated as a published construction of every detail of current § 602.1(b). But the same intentional-interference/obstruction-or-intimidation structure appears in current subdivision (b), and Dubner is binding Ninth Circuit authority on the Fourth Amendment probable-cause consequences of treating refusal alone as sufficient under § 602.1.

    California's published Hamburg v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc., 116 Cal.App.4th 497 (2004) reaches a closely related point under § 602.1(a): violating or refusing to accept otherwise enforceable time/place/manner rules does not necessarily constitute the misdemeanor. The criminal § 602.1 question remains whether the statutory obstruction/intimidation/interference elements occurred. Hamburg involved private retail property, not a public-agency interior, so it does not decide the forum or policy authority of a government facility. It does strongly support keeping was the rule enforceable? separate from were the elements of § 602.1 committed?

    A later unpublished Ninth Circuit decision, Mackey v. Meyer, 675 F. App'x 705 (9th Cir. 2017), applied Dubner specifically to § 602.1(b) at a California DMV and found no probable cause where video contradicted assertions of obstruction/intimidation. Because that disposition is unpublished, this guide retains it only as a research cross-check, not binding precedent.

    Field rule: Do not concede that I refused to leave or I violated policy supplies the missing obstruction/intimidation facts. Ask what conduct is alleged to constitute intentional interference and separately identify the authority for the underlying policy/exclusion.

    Case sources:

    • Dubner v. City & County of San Francisco, 266 F.3d 959 (9th Cir. 2001): https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2004/03/17/9917319.pdf

    § 602.1(d)(2) — constitutionally protected activity is expressly excluded¶

    Current § 602.1 contains an additional limitation that is especially important in public-facility recording disputes. Subdivision (d)(2) states that the section shall not apply to a person on the premises who is engaging in activities protected by the California Constitution or the United States Constitution.

    This means § 602.1 cannot be used by simply assuming that the expressive activity being challenged is criminal interference. The analysis should identify the activity first and ask whether it is constitutionally protected on the actual facts.

    The Ninth Circuit expressly noticed this statutory structure in Center for Bio-Ethical Reform, Inc. v. Los Angeles County Sheriff's Department, 533 F.3d 780 (9th Cir. 2008), contrasting statutes such as § 602.1 that use shall not apply language for constitutionally protected activity with a broader school-statute savings clause. The court's discussion does not itself decide whether a particular activity is protected, but it confirms that the carveout is substantive statutory text rather than surplusage.

    A useful public-agency sequence is therefore:

    identify the activity → determine whether it is constitutionally protected on these facts → apply § 602.1(d)(2) if protected → identify any additional conduct → test obstruction/intimidation and refusal elements → consider any independently applicable law

    The carveout is not immunity for independently unprotected conduct. A person who is speaking, petitioning, observing, or recording can still engage in separate obstruction, intimidation, threats, unreasonable disruption, or other unlawful acts. Section 602.1(e) also expressly preserves the application of other law.

    A nonbinding federal habeas case, Sherman v. Yolo County Sheriff (E.D. Cal. 2010), illustrates that distinction. The record supporting the library § 602.1(b) conviction included evidence of rule violations, verbally abusive conduct, frightened patrons leaving, and repeated refusal to leave. The court did not treat simultaneous assertions of constitutional rights as immunizing independently disruptive conduct. That case is retained only as a factual cross-check, not controlling construction of subdivision (d)(2).

    Local police-lobby comparator — Wilson v. County of Contra Costa¶

    Wilson v. County of Contra Costa, No. 14-cv-03491-SI (N.D. Cal. May 6, 2015) is a particularly close California facility comparator. At the motion-to-dismiss stage, the court allowed a First Amendment retaliation claim to proceed where the plaintiff alleged that he was recording officers performing their duties in the public lobby of the Contra Costa County Sheriff's Office and was arrested after an officer noticed the recording.

    The same order rejected § 602.1(b) as an established probable-cause basis on the pleaded facts, which included allegations that officers physically blocked the plaintiff from leaving and that he had difficulty standing and walking. The court did not hold that § 602.1(b) can never apply in a sheriff/police lobby, and it expressly recognized that later evidence might show intentional interference and refusal.

    Wilson is a federal district-court pleading-stage decision, not binding Ninth Circuit precedent, and the case did not reach a final merits adjudication of the surviving recording claim. Its proper use is narrow but valuable:

    • recording officers in a public law-enforcement lobby is not automatically equivalent to § 602.1 interference;
    • refusal/remaining must still be separated from the statute's obstruction/intimidation predicate;
    • the protected-activity question and any additional unprotected conduct must be analyzed separately.

    Updated field rule: For § 602.1 at a public agency, do not stop at policy violation or refusal to leave. Identify the alleged obstruction/intimidation, test the current subdivision (d)(2) constitutional-activity carveout, then identify any independent law that might still apply.

    § 602.1(c) — knowingly material misrepresentation of law¶

    This separate public-agency provision is easy to overlook. It applies where a person intentionally interferes with public-agency business by knowingly making a material misrepresentation of the law to people there to transact business with the agency, followed by refusal to leave after the qualifying request.

    The 2017 legislative materials describe the amendment as aimed at a then-perceived gap involving illegitimate solicitors who made material misrepresentations of law to people transacting business with public agencies. The statute still must be applied according to its enacted elements.

    Field implication: The project should be meticulous about stating law accurately. A good-faith disagreement over legal interpretation is not textually identical to knowingly making a material misrepresentation, but controlling judicial construction of subdivision (c) remains to be researched.

    § 602.1(e) — other law still matters¶

    Section 602.1 does not supersede other law. Therefore:

    I do not satisfy § 602.1(b) is not the same proposition as no other lawful authority can require me to leave.

    That distinction is central to the Field Guide.

    § 602.6 — why it matters even though it is specialized¶

    Section 602.6 is a useful antidote to vague claims that California always requires a person in a government facility to identify themselves. It is a specific statute for specified fair buildings/grounds when they are not open to the general public, after a qualifying direction to leave and determination that the person lacks apparent lawful business or another legitimate reason for remaining; the statute then expressly includes failure to identify and account for one's presence.

    That specialized text should not be generalized into a statewide public employee asks for ID → ID is mandatory rule.

    Live removal / trespass decision tree¶

    This is a factual and legal sorting tool, not a set of magic words.

    1. Establish the status of the specific area¶

    Do not classify the entire building with one label if the encounter occurs in only part of it. Ask/document:

    • Is this government property?
    • What specific area am I in: exterior sidewalk/plaza, vestibule, public lobby, service counter area, meeting room, secured hallway, staff-only area, courtroom, records room, etc.?
    • Is this particular area presently open to the general public?
    • Is the building within normal public hours?
    • Is this area restricted/secured/staff-only even though another part of the building is public-facing?
    • Is there a posted closure, screening requirement, visitor sign-in rule, badge rule, appointment rule, or other access condition?

    This immediately separates provisions such as § 602(o), § 602(q), and § 602(y).

    Do not equate public access with public-forum status. A lobby can be open for agency business while remaining a nonpublic or limited-purpose forum. Forum classification is a separate constitutional inquiry and may differ from one area of the same facility to another.

    2. Identify the rule being invoked¶

    If an employee says policy says no filming, you must sign in, or you must show ID, distinguish:

    • the written facility policy;
    • whether the rule is a screening rule, visitor-management condition, or identification demand;
    • any statute/regulation/order authorizing the restriction;
    • the forum/access classification of the specific area;
    • any applicable constitutional limitation; and
    • the criminal statute, if police are threatening arrest.

    A policy can be legally valid without itself being the criminal offense. Conversely, calling something policy does not immunize it from constitutional review. Hamburg is a useful statutory reminder that enforceability of a rule and commission of a § 602.1 crime are separate questions.

    For planned facility investigations, Facility Research and Field Verification provides the project workflow for tracing the authority chain from controlling law to written policy, signage, prior enforcement, exclusion/removal authority, and any separate detention/citation/arrest authority invoked.

    3. Identify the alleged conduct¶

    Useful questions include:

    • What specific conduct are you saying violates the rule?
    • Is the asserted violation simply that I am recording?
    • Is the asserted violation that I declined to sign a visitor log or show identification?
    • Is this a security-screening requirement, or a separate visitor-access policy?
    • Who am I allegedly obstructing or intimidating?
    • What agency business am I intentionally interfering with?
    • Are you saying this particular area is not open to the general public?
    • Are you saying the building is currently closed?

    If § 602.1 is asserted, Dubner makes the distinction between refusal and intentional interference especially important.

    4. If police arrive, separate removal authority from detention authority¶

    Ask:

    • Am I being detained?
    • What offense do you reasonably suspect?
    • Which Penal Code section/subdivision are you relying on?
    • What facts satisfy its elements?

    A facility employee's desire that someone leave and a peace officer's authority to detain/arrest are analytically distinct questions.

    Edgerly is also a useful reminder that probable cause must ultimately connect to a particular statutory offense. An officer can rely on a different offense from the one first announced if the known facts objectively establish it, so the correct field question is not merely Did you cite the wrong subsection? but What actual offense do the known facts establish?

    5. Treat ID as a separate legal question¶

    Do not provide false identifying information. Penal Code § 148.9 is separately queued for the police-encounters/identification chapter.

    If an officer or employee asserts that identification is mandatory, identify the legal basis rather than assuming a universal California stop-and-identify or government-building sign-in rule. The existence of specialized statutes such as § 602.6 and § 602(y) reinforces why the precise legal setting matters.

    6. If the encounter reaches a final arrest threat¶

    A useful evidentiary question is:

    Are you ordering me to leave under threat of arrest, and what law are you relying on?

    If the person chooses to comply, they can make clear that compliance is under protest and not consent to the asserted legal basis. If the person chooses to remain, the risk changes substantially because an officer may arrest first and the legal merits may be litigated later.

    The Field Guide's purpose is to help a person understand that choice and preserve the factual/legal record, not to pretend a correct legal argument guarantees that an arrest will not occur.

    What this review does not yet establish¶

    This chapter does not yet establish final doctrine on:

    • application of § 602(m) specifically to ordinary government-property interiors open to the public, beyond the established nontransient/continuous-occupation requirement;
    • the full California appellate construction of § 602(o)'s not open to the general public limitation or the scope of its constitutional-savings language;
    • constitutional limits on removing a peaceful recorder from government-building interiors;
    • forum classification of libraries, police lobbies, DA/reception lobbies, agency counters, DMV lobbies, courthouses, and similar facilities;
    • whether and when visitor sign-in, badge, appointment, or ID conditions are lawful for a particular government-office area;
    • whether and when violation of a government-facility policy supplies lawful grounds for exclusion;
    • due-process issues surrounding longer-term bans/exclusions;
    • First Amendment retaliation where protected recording or criticism motivates removal;
    • the full current construction of § 602.1(b)'s obstruction/intimidation element in public-agency interiors;
    • § 602.1(c)'s material-misrepresentation provision;
    • identification duties during lawful detention;
    • local ordinances or agency regulations that independently govern a facility.

    The next doctrinal pass should prioritize:

    • California appellate cases applying § 602(o) to public/government property;
    • § 602.1(b) public-agency obstruction/intimidation cases;
