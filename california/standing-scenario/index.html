    Standing Scenario¶

    The Field Guide uses one default factual baseline so each hypothetical can analyze the government's asserted authority without inventing unrelated misconduct.

    The project-methodology status describes this page's function. Statutory and case-law propositions referenced here retain the verification status shown in the substantive chapters, authority index, and sweep ledgers.

    Unless a chapter or scenario expressly changes a fact, assume the factual conditions below. The baseline does not decide whether a disputed recording restriction, expressive activity, facility rule, or later government order is lawful; those legal questions are analyzed separately.

    Baseline facts¶

    At the beginning of the scenario, assume the auditor:

    1. is in a place and specific area they may lawfully be, during hours when that area is open to the public;
    2. has not entered an employee-only, secured, closed, or otherwise restricted area;
    3. is peacefully observing, photographing, filming, recording, asking questions, requesting records, or otherwise gathering information;
    4. is not blocking doors, counters, queues, sidewalks, roadways, traffic, or another person's movement;
    5. is not physically interfering with employees, officers, emergency personnel, visitors, or members of the public;
    6. is not touching, crowding, following at close range, or entering another person's personal space;
    7. is not threatening anyone, fighting, or challenging anyone to fight;
    8. is not creating loud and unreasonable noise or intentionally shouting over government business;
    9. is not intentionally obstructing or intimidating employees or people transacting business with a public agency, and is not knowingly making a material misrepresentation of law to people there to transact business with the agency;
    10. is not willfully resisting, delaying, or obstructing an officer through conduct independent of the activity being analyzed;
    11. is not gathered with others to commit an unlawful act or to carry out a lawful act in a violent, boisterous, or tumultuous manner; is not remaining at a riot, rout, or unlawful assembly after a lawful warning to disperse; and is not assembled with others for the purpose of disturbing the public peace or committing an unlawful act;
    12. has no additional misconduct silently added to the scenario; if an analysis depends on some other act, omission, intent, or circumstance, that fact must be expressly supplied;
    13. is not intentionally targeting uninvolved private individuals or their personal information; and
    14. is not assumed to carry any identification card or credential merely by virtue of being present in public; and
    15. does not initiate confrontational interaction with employees, officers, visitors, or private individuals. If another person voluntarily approaches, questions, criticizes, follows, or argues with the auditor, that person's choice to engage is not silently converted into misconduct by the auditor.

    The auditor may criticize government, decline consensual conversation, decline consent to a search, ask officials to identify legal authority, or continue recording while remaining in the same lawful physical location. Those facts are not silently converted into obstruction, intimidation, disturbance, or disorderly conduct. Whether the recording or expression itself is protected in that specific place remains a separate legal question.

    An uninvolved private individual is not made a target merely by being present. If that person voluntarily engages the auditor or becomes directly relevant to the government conduct being documented, the resulting interaction may itself be documented; that does not authorize unnecessary pursuit of unrelated personal information.

    The standing scenario does not create a universal rule that identification can never be required. Custody/booking, specialized facilities, and other specific legal contexts may create different duties. For ordinary pedestrian police encounters, see police-encounters.md: the current California sweep has not identified a general pedestrian carry-ID statute or general statutory stop-and-identify law equivalent to Nevada's law in Hiibel. An officer's request for identification does not itself establish a legal duty; the applicable statute, context, and case law still have to be identified.

    What the standing facts are designed to exclude¶

    The standing scenario does not create immunity from any statute. It removes the common factual predicates that otherwise derail analysis of the legal issue actually presented.

    Authority / label Standing-scenario consequence
    Penal Code § 415 — disturbing the peace No fighting or challenge to fight is assumed; no malicious/willful loud and unreasonable noise is assumed; no words satisfying § 415(3)'s immediate-violent-reaction element are assumed.
    Penal Code § 403 — disturbing a meeting No willful disturbance or breaking up of a lawful meeting is assumed. In a meeting scenario, a brief verbal or nonverbal reaction is not assumed to actually impede proceedings unless the facts say otherwise.
    Penal Code § 148(a)(1) — resisting/delaying/obstructing No independent willful resistance, delay, or obstruction is assumed. Section 148(g) separately provides that recording a public/peace officer in the statutory circumstances does not, by itself, establish § 148(a), reasonable suspicion, or probable cause.
    Penal Code §§ 602 / 602.1 — trespass/interference The auditor is not assumed to begin the encounter as a trespasser, to obstruct or intimidate public-agency business, or to knowingly make a material misrepresentation of law to people there to transact business with the agency. A later order to leave is a new fact that must be analyzed rather than presumed valid or invalid.
    Penal Code §§ 407-409 / 416 — unlawful assembly, participation, and dispersal theories No riot, rout, unlawful assembly, participation in an unlawful assembly, or gathering formed for an unlawful/disturbance purpose is assumed.
    Penal Code § 647 — disorderly conduct None of § 647's enumerated forms of disorderly conduct is silently assumed merely because someone calls the auditor "disorderly," "suspicious," or "annoying."
    Local ordinance / facility rule No unstated noise, obstruction, restricted-area entry, loitering, or similar conduct is assumed. The actual ordinance, regulation, policy, or rule must be identified and analyzed.

    Another person's reaction is not silently attributed to the auditor¶

    A government employee, contractor, security guard, visitor, or private individual may become uncomfortable, angry, offended, argumentative, or confrontational because of the peaceful activity described by the standing facts.

    Unless the scenario says otherwise, that reaction does not change the baseline facts about what the auditor personally did.

    The guide therefore should not reason backward from:

    someone became upset

    to:

    the auditor must have been disturbing the peace, obstructing business, intimidating someone, or causing a meeting disruption.

    The relevant statute still has to be matched to the auditor's own conduct.

    Subjective complaint conversion: labels are not facts¶

    A recurring enforcement failure can occur when another person's reaction is converted into an objective-sounding accusation:

    peaceful activity → objection or anger → "harassment," "disruption," "interference," "intimidation," or "disturbance" → official or police enforcement

    Those labels do not supply missing statutory or policy elements. When one is asserted, strip the conclusion away and record the observable conduct:

    • who approached whom;
    • who followed whom, if anyone;
    • distance and physical positioning;
    • exact words and volume;
    • whether anyone blocked movement or business;
    • whether the auditor attempted to disengage;
    • what rule or offense was identified;
    • what element each alleged fact is supposed to satisfy; and
    • what contemporaneous video, witnesses, CCTV, CAD, body-camera, or other evidence bears on the allegation.

    This project calls that workflow the Allegation-to-Elements Check. "Subjective complaint conversion" is an analytical label for a failure mode, not a freestanding legal doctrine.

    Public-meeting variant¶

    For a Brown Act meeting, also assume unless stated otherwise:

    • the auditor obeys otherwise lawful time limits and procedural rules governing the speaker's own comment period;
    • a brief expression such as applause, laughter, a gesture, or a short verbal reaction does not actually disrupt, disturb, impede, or render infeasible the orderly conduct of the meeting;
    • the auditor does not threaten force;
    • any favorable and unfavorable audience reactions are documented separately so viewpoint-neutrality questions can be tested against comparable conduct.

    Government Code § 54957.95 currently defines "disrupting" in terms of behavior that actually disrupts, disturbs, impedes, or renders infeasible the orderly conduct of the meeting and ordinarily requires a warning before removal. Section 54954.3 separately prohibits a local legislative body from prohibiting public criticism of agency policies, programs, services, or the acts or omissions of the legislative body.

    The public-meeting assumption does not itself decide whether a particular reasonable and lawful decorum regulation applies. That question remains part of the statutory and First Amendment analysis.

    New facts restart the analysis¶

    The standing scenario is a starting point, not a prediction of the outcome.

    Examples of facts that require a fresh legal analysis include:

    • an employee or supervisor orders the auditor to leave;
    • a facility claims an area is restricted;
    • normal public hours end;
    • a security-screening condition is invoked;
    • an officer establishes a safety perimeter;
    • an officer says the auditor is detained;
    • an ordinance, regulation, court rule, or facility policy is identified;
    • the auditor changes position or conduct;
    • a meeting chair gives a warning under an applicable rule;
    • the government identifies conduct independent of filming or criticism that it claims is unlawful.

    A later removal command does not retroactively make the initial lawful presence trespass, and the standing scenario does not predetermine whether refusing that later command would be lawful. That question belongs to the applicable forum, policy, removal, trespass, and police-encounter analysis.

    Why this matters¶

    Without a fixed baseline, a hypothetical about a camera can become a hypothetical about imaginary shouting; a hypothetical about a records request can become one about imaginary obstruction; and a hypothetical about removal can quietly assume the auditor was already trespassing.

    The standing scenario prevents that drift. If a legal conclusion depends on additional misconduct, the scenario must actually supply that fact.
